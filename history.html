The Foundation for A-Level Success

The evolution of maths

Maths did not appear all at once. It grew as people counted possessions, measured land, predicted the seasons, built cities, studied the stars, and searched for patterns in the world.

From counting to computing

A living subject

Each era added a new way to describe, predict, or prove something about the world.

Before 3000 BCE
01

Prehistoric counting

Numbers begin as marks

Long before formal numerals, people tracked animals, seasons, and exchanges with notches on bones, stones, and sticks. These marks were a practical memory aid: one mark could stand for one sheep, one day, or one traded object.

Big idea: Maths begins when a quantity is separated from the object being counted.

c. 3000 BCE
02

Mesopotamia

Writing makes calculation permanent

Sumerian and Babylonian scribes recorded accounts on clay tablets. Their base-60 system was powerful for trade, astronomy, and measurement. Our 60 seconds in a minute and 360 degrees in a circle still echo this choice.

New tools: Place value, multiplication tables, fractions, and early quadratic problem solving.

c. 2000 BCE
03

Ancient Egypt

Geometry serves the river and the city

Egyptian mathematics grew from surveying fields after the Nile flooded, collecting taxes, designing buildings, and managing grain. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus preserves methods for fractions, areas, volumes, and practical equations.

New tools: Unit fractions, rules for areas, volume calculations, and approximations of pi.

c. 600-300 BCE
04

Ancient Greece

Proof changes maths into a system

Greek thinkers shifted attention from only getting an answer to explaining why an answer must be true. Euclid organised geometry from definitions and axioms, while Pythagoras and others explored number relationships, proportion, and shape.

New tools: Deductive proof, logical definitions, geometric theorems, and irrational numbers.

c. 250 BCE
05

Archimedes

Mathematics measures the impossible

Archimedes used increasingly fine approximations to estimate areas, volumes, and the value of pi. His exhaustion method anticipated the central idea of calculus: approximate a complicated object with many simpler ones.

New tools: Limits by approximation, centres of mass, levers, and improved estimates of pi.

c. 200 BCE-200 CE
06

China and India

Algorithms make arithmetic portable

Chinese mathematicians developed systematic methods for solving equations with counting rods. In India, the decimal place-value system matured, making large calculations far more efficient and preparing the way for the modern zero.

New tools: Negative numbers, efficient algorithms, decimal place value, and the idea of zero as a number.

c. 820 CE
07

Islamic Golden Age

Algebra gets its name

In Baghdad, scholars translated and extended Greek, Indian, and Persian work. Al-Khwarizmi described systematic ways to solve equations; the word algorithm comes from the Latin form of his name, while algebra comes from the title of his work.

New tools: Systematic algebra, Hindu-Arabic numerals, trigonometric tables, and mathematical astronomy.

1202
08

Medieval Europe

Numerals travel through trade

Leonardo of Pisa, known as Fibonacci, introduced European readers to the Hindu-Arabic number system in Liber Abaci. The system was not adopted immediately, but its place value made commercial calculation much faster than Roman numerals.

Turning point: Written calculation becomes quicker, more flexible, and easier to teach.

1500s-1600s
09

Renaissance Europe

Symbols unlock general rules

Mathematicians began using symbols consistently for unknowns, operations, and powers. Cardano and others developed methods for cubic and quartic equations, while Descartes connected algebra to geometry using coordinates.

New tools: Symbolic algebra, coordinates, negative solutions, and the link between equations and curves.

1680s
10

Newton and Leibniz

Calculus describes change

Newton and Leibniz developed calculus independently to study motion, curves, areas, and changing quantities. Differentiation measures instantaneous change; integration combines infinitely many small pieces to find a total.

New tools: Limits, derivatives, integrals, and the fundamental connection between them.

1700s-1800s
11

Probability and statistics

Uncertainty becomes measurable

Work on games of chance grew into probability theory. Statistics then gave governments, scientists, and businesses ways to summarise data, estimate populations, and reason about patterns that are never perfectly predictable.

New tools: Expected value, distributions, averages, sampling, correlation, and inference.

1800s
12

New geometries

Maths expands beyond the familiar plane

Lobachevsky, Bolyai, Riemann, and others showed that changing Euclid's assumptions creates coherent new geometries. This opened mathematics to curved spaces and later became important in physics and relativity.

New tools: Non-Euclidean geometry, abstract spaces, tensors, and mathematical models of curvature.

1900s
13

Logic and foundations

Mathematicians question what proof means

Set theory, formal logic, and new axiomatic systems examined the foundations of mathematics. Gödel later showed that sufficiently powerful formal systems contain truths that cannot be proved within the system itself.

New tools: Formal languages, set theory, mathematical logic, and rigorous definitions of proof.

1940s-present
14

The computer age

Algorithms turn ideas into action

Electronic computers made it possible to perform enormous calculations and simulate systems such as weather, markets, galaxies, and protein structures. Computer science grew from logic, number theory, algorithms, and the study of information.

New tools: Binary numbers, algorithms, cryptography, numerical modelling, data science, and machine learning.

Now and next
15

Mathematics in progress

The story is still being written

Modern maths continues to connect previously separate ideas. Researchers study climate, networks, quantum systems, cryptography, artificial intelligence, and the mathematics of biological life.

Your place in the story: Every new problem gives mathematics another question to describe, test, and solve.
